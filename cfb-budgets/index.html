Money gets you in the poll. It doesn’t decide where you land.

Seen from pug height, a roster budget is a very large bowl of kibble and the AP Top 25 is the treat jar. The pugs lined up estimated 2026 budgets for 68 programs against the poll. All ten of the biggest spenders are ranked, but seven of the biggest 25 are not. Houston, Iowa and Utah made the poll on the three smallest budgets of any ranked team. The pugs also asked whether a dog nickname helps. It doesn’t. The last section audits the data.

What these numbers can’t tell you

  • Correlation, not cause. Winning also raises budgets through donations and revenue, and prestige, recruiting and coaching affect both.
  • The line fits ranked teams only. AP ranks exist for 25 teams, so the slope says nothing about how unranked teams would order, and cutting off at 25 tends to flatten it. The ranked-versus-unranked comparison uses all 68.
  • Small sample, not fully independent. It is one season. Teams in the same conference, and teams that play each other, aren’t independent, so the p-values and intervals are somewhat optimistic.
  • Estimated budgets. Random noise in the estimates usually weakens a real relationship. A systematic bias in the estimates wouldn’t show up in the range test.
  • Not a random sample of programs. These 68 teams are mostly power-conference schools, so the results don’t describe all of college football.
  • Exploratory. These checks were chosen after looking at the data, with no correction for multiple tests. They all ask the same question and the main p-values are far below 0.05, but treat the exact values loosely.

Pug question: Do dogs beat cats?

Dogs, cats or neither, a nickname doesn’t put you in the poll

The pugs sorted the 68 teams by nickname. Four have a dog-family nickname (Bulldogs, Huskies, Wolfpack), 12 have a cat-family one (Tigers, Wildcats, Cougars, Panthers, Nittany Lions) and 52 have neither. One of the four dogs is ranked (Georgia, #2), against five of the 12 cats and 19 of the other 52. The highest-ranked cat is LSU, at #7. Average budgets are within $1.5M of each other: $26.8M for dogs, $26.0M for cats and $27.5M for the rest. The tick marks each group’s average.

Fisher’s exact test: dogs against cats, p = 1.0; cats against everyone else, p = 0.75. Four dogs can only reveal a very large gap, and there isn’t one. The pugs are not sulking.

Pug question: So what did the pugs learn?

A big bowl gets you to the treat jar, not to the top of it

  • The bowl matters. Ranked teams’ budgets average $13.1M more than unranked teams’ (95% interval $8.5M to $17.6M), and each extra $10M multiplies the odds of being ranked by about 5.5 (2.4 to 12.4).
  • The bowl isn’t destiny. Among ranked teams, budget accounts for about a third of the differences in rank (R² 0.36), and seven of the 25 biggest spenders aren’t ranked.
  • Some dogs punch above their weight. Georgia, Indiana and BYU ranked far above their budgets, and Houston, Iowa and Utah made the poll on the three smallest budgets of any ranked team.
  • A dog nickname doesn’t help. One of four dog teams is ranked, against five of 12 cat teams and 19 of 52 others, and the difference is well within chance.
  • The pugs can’t see over the rim. Budgets are estimated ranges, so gaps of a few million between teams mean little.
