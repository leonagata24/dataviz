Things in the Sky That Are Not Squirrels

Seen from pug height, a hot air balloon is a very large, very slow ball that nobody will throw. The Hot Air Balloon Database lists 7,235 of them: who built each one, when, how big, who flies it and what it is called. This page asks what pugs would ask: how long a balloon lasts, who makes them, whether they are getting bigger, what people name them, and how many anyone has met in person. The file needed repairs first, and the full sniff test is at the bottom.

Flight 01 · 6,920 balloons with a build year

How long before a toy ends up in the closet?

About 35 yearsA balloon built in the early 1990s is a coin flip: 52% of them are still listed as in service. From the 1970s it is 20%; from the 2010s, 93%.

Our toys last four minutes. Respect.

Group the balloons by the year they were built and count how many the archive still lists as in service. The share falls steadily with age and crosses one half between the 1991 and 1992 builds, roughly 35 years old today. For the 1,052 balloons built from 1990 to 1994 it is 52%, with a 95% range of 49% to 55%, so "half" is a fair reading and not a rounding trick. There is no cliff: 34% of late-1980s builds and 74% of early-2000s builds are still flying, so retirement looks gradual rather than tied to a fixed lifespan.

This is a snapshot, not a true survival curve. An old balloon scrapped decades ago is less likely to have been entered at all, which would make old balloons look more durable than they were, and "in service" is the archive's label with no date attached. The result does not hinge on the oddities in the file: leaving out rebuilt envelopes gives 50% for 1990 to 1994, and US-based balloons alone give 54%.

Flight 02 · US-based balloons only

Who makes the giant balls now?

Changed handsAerostar/Raven built 40% of the 1990s balloons in the US part of the archive and none after 2008. Czech-made Kubicek went from 0.6% of 2000s builds to 32% of 2020s builds, the largest share of any maker.

The old toy factory closed. A new one across the big water took over. We will accept either.

Among balloons based in the USA, three US-based makers account for most of the 1990s: Aerostar/Raven, Cameron US and Firefly/Balloon Works. Two of them fade. The last Aerostar/Raven in the file is dated 2008, and Firefly/Balloon Works falls from 18% of 1990s builds to 1.3% of 2020s builds. Lindstrand US arrives in 1995 and holds roughly a fifth from the 2000s on. Kubicek's lead in the 2020s is clear of sampling noise: its share has a 95% range of 29% to 35%, against 18% for Lindstrand US in second place. Kubicek and Spain's Ultramagic together make up 39% of US-based balloons built in the 2020s.

These are shares of balloons in the archive by build year, not sales figures. Older balloons that were retired and never cataloged are missing, so early decades are the least reliable.

    Flight 03 · 6,749 balloons with a size

    Is the bed getting bigger?

    Only the big onesThe typical balloon has not grown in 35 years: the median is 90,000 cubic feet in every period since 1990. The big end has doubled. One in ten early-1990s builds was 105,400 cubic feet or more; for 2020 to 2024 that mark is 210,000.

    Same bed for us. Much bigger sofa for the guests.

    Size here is envelope volume. 1,688 balloons, 25% of those with a size, are exactly 90,000 cubic feet, and that standard has not moved. What changed is the share of very large balloons: envelopes of 150,000 cubic feet or more were 3.8% of early-1990s builds (95% range 2.8% to 5.2%) and 16.3% of 2020 to 2024 builds (13.7% to 19.4%). The ranges do not come close to overlapping, and the same rise shows among US-based balloons alone (2.9% to 17.1%).

    The file does not say what a balloon is used for, but the owners give a hint: the names that appear most on these 474 large envelopes are ride companies, led by Rainbow Ryders (42), Sundance Balloons (29) and Hot Air Expeditions (18). The climb is not smooth, because sizes come in standard classes and the 90th percentile jumps between them: it dipped to 150,000 for 2015 to 2019 builds before returning to 210,000.

    Flight 04 · 4,840 balloons with a name

    Are there any dogs up there? And do rescues keep their name?

    OutnumberedAmong single animals, bears lead the sky with 30 names, then cats with 18 and ducks with 15. Dogs have 13, three of them bulldogs. And a balloon rarely keeps its name: about 7 in 10 past owners with a recorded name called it something different from today's.

    Cats. In the sky. Above us. We would like to speak to whoever is in charge.

    Two thirds of balloons (67%) have a recorded name. Searching those names for animal words gives a rough census: it counts the word, so "Stray Cat" and "Legal Eagle" count whether or not the balloon looks like the animal, and puns slip through. Luke Skybarker, a special shape once named Beagle Maximus, is not in the dog count. Read each number as a floor, and do not read much into the order below the top three: 13 dogs, 12 fish, 11 bees and 11 horses are too close to rank. Birds counted as one family, on a looser word list, would come to roughly 85 and beat every single animal. One balloon in the archive has a pug in its name: S.S. Rollo Fitzpug, an Aerostar/Raven from 1995 in Illinois, listed as out of service.

    Names also show who pays for balloons. The most common name is RE/MAX, on 63 balloons, followed by the ride company Rainbow Ryders. After the sponsors come the words balloonists reach for themselves: blue, rainbow, dream, spirit, sunrise.

    When a balloon changes hands, the name usually changes too. The archive records 5,169 past owners, and for 1,622 of them both the old name and today's name are known. On an exact match, 72% differ. Counting near-matches as kept (a respelling, or a name that survives as one of two alternates) brings it to 68%, so "about 7 in 10" holds either way. Two cautions: the old name is known for just 31% of past owners, and a changed name may be more likely to get written down than an unchanged one, so the true rate could be lower.

    Flight 05 · the archive's own logbook

    Has anyone actually sniffed these?

    One in sixWhoever keeps this log has seen 1,199 of the 7,235 balloons in person (16.6%), crewed for 68, worked the burner on 35 and flown in 24. A single year, 2023, accounts for 813 sightings.

    We only trust what we have sniffed. By that rule, this person trusts 1,199 balloons. That is a lot of sniffing.

    Five columns in the file are not about balloons at all. They record one person's encounters, and they show how a balloonist's time is spent: mostly looking, sometimes holding a rope, rarely in the basket. The sightings start in 2008 at a few dozen a year, skip 2016 and 2020 almost entirely, then jump from 144 in 2022 to 813 in 2023 before settling at 247 in 2024. The file does not say why. New Mexico, home of the Albuquerque fiesta, has the most seen balloons (252), followed by Ohio (115).

    It also explains part of the sniff test. A balloon the logkeeper has seen has a recorded name 94% of the time; one they have not, 62%. The gap is not just a matter of American balloons being better documented: among US-based balloons alone it is 94% against 65%.

    Before you quote any of this

    What this can and can't tell you

    • It describes an archive, not the world. Entries arrive through public contributions and one community's attention. A country with few entries may simply have few contributors.
    • The chart the pugs refused to draw. Special shapes are 25% of UK entries and 4.9% of US entries. That is almost certainly because a foreign balloon gets cataloged when it is remarkable, not because a quarter of British balloons are shaped like something. Any country comparison has this problem.
    • Status has no date. "In service" and "out of service" are the archive's labels at an unknown moment, and 87 balloons are unknown.
    • Blank is not zero. Missing previous owners, names and sizes mean not recorded. The renaming rate rests on the minority of handovers where both names were written down.
    • Name counts are keyword counts. They were checked by eye for false matches (the cow count was dropped because of "Moon"), but they miss puns and shapes with unrelated names, and the small counts are too close to rank.
    • Ranges are sampling ranges only. The 95% ranges quoted treat the archive as a sample. They say nothing about which balloons were never entered, which is the larger uncertainty.
    Sniff test · 7,934 rows in the file

    Does this data smell right?

    Edible, with careThe file is broken as delivered but repairable, and once repaired it is tidy. Its real limit is coverage: this is one enthusiast community's archive, 79% American, not a census of the world's balloons. Counts by country and anything about the ownership trail outside the USA were left alone.

    Smells like one human's backpack. A good backpack. Still one backpack.

    Opened naively, the sheet has 7,934 data rows, and the most common "registration" is the word No. The cause: line breaks inside the previous-owners field spilled 699 fragments onto rows of their own, pushing the photo link and the four yes/no flags of 412 balloons into the wrong columns. Every one of those chains ends in exactly one row carrying the displaced values, so stitching them back is unambiguous. It gives 7,235 balloons with IDs that run from 1 to 7,235 with no gaps and no repeats.
