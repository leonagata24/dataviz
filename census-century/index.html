The first century of the U.S. Census · 1790–1900

From 3.9 million to 76 million

Twelve censuses, 49 states and territories, and a population that grew nineteen-fold while its centre of gravity walked about 500 miles west. This page checks the data, reads the 1883 original, and rebuilds it four ways.

Step 1 · Data check

The file is clean. The borders are not.

Structurally, nothing needs fixing: no blanks, no duplicates, consistent names, and national totals that match the official census counts to the person. The issues are historical. The data assigns every count to today’s state lines, and it counts places that were not yet states. Both are kept, and both are now flagged.

CheckResultFinding and treatment
StructurePass886 rows × 3 columns (State, Year, Population). 0 missing values, 0 duplicate state-year pairs, integer populations, 51 consistently spelled names with no stray whitespace.
ScopeFilteredThe file runs to 1990 (21 censuses). The exercise covers the first century, so the page uses the 12 censuses from 1790 to 1900: 437 rows.
Totals reconcilePassSummed state counts equal the published national totals: 3,929,214 (1790), 23,191,876 (1850), 75,994,575 (1900).
Modern bordersNoteMaine is counted apart from Massachusetts in 1790 (it separated in 1820), and West Virginia apart from Virginia (1863). Kentucky and Tennessee appear in 1790 as their later state areas. This is why ranks here differ from the 1883 plate, which put Massachusetts 2nd in 1790. Here it is 4th.
Not yet statesAddedTerritories and pre-statehood areas are mixed in with states (e.g. Alabama, 1,250 people in 1800). A status field was added by comparing each admission date with census day. In 1790, 13 of 18 entries were states; in 1900, 45 of 49. D.C. is flagged separately.
Missing areasNoteAlaska (enumerated from 1880) and Hawaii (from 1900) are absent before 1960, so totals describe the contiguous U.S. Unorganised and Indian lands not enumerated in early censuses are also absent. That is a gap in the source, not the file.
OutliersRealJumps such as Minnesota +2,731% (1850–60) or North Dakota +1,435% (1870–80) are frontier growth from tiny bases. Nevada’s fall from 62,266 (1880) to 42,335 (1900) is the silver bust. All verified as plausible, none altered.
Known count qualityNoteThe 1870 census is known to have undercounted the South. Published figures are used as-is; growth rates for 1860–70 and 1870–80 in Southern states should be read with that in mind.
EnrichmentAddedRank per census, Census Bureau region (Northeast, South, Midwest, West), admission date, and a geographic centroid per state. Centroids give an estimated centre of population; it lands within about 0.5° of the Census Bureau’s official points.
Step 2 · The original

A rank chart drawn by hand in 1883

What it does well

  • One frame shows every state’s rank at every census. The bump chart was a striking idea for 1883.
  • The left column lists admission dates, so newcomers can be matched to statehood.
  • Each box carries the count and density, so the numbers are there for anyone who looks closely.

Where it struggles

  • Rank hides magnitude. The gap between #1 and #2 is drawn the same as #37 to #38, even when one gap is a million people and the other a few thousand.
  • One colour, hundreds of crossings. Following a single state means tracing a red line through a thicket.
  • Geography is implied. The westward story only appears if you already know where Ohio and Iowa are.
  • Growth is invisible. A state can grow 50% and still drop a rank.

How the makeovers respond

  • A: the same rank chart, coloured by region, with a switch to true population.
  • B: a map with the centre of population, so “west” is something you see.
  • C: regional shares, the balance of the country in one area chart.
  • D: decade growth rates, showing the frontier as waves.

Step 4 · What the data says

Four findings

New York took the lead early

Virginia ranked 1st in 1790 and 1800. New York passed it in 1810 and held 1st for the rest of the century, reaching 7.3 million by 1900.

The Old Northwest rose fastest

Illinois went from 2,458 people in 1800 (24th) to 4.8 million and 3rd place in 1900. Ohio reached 3rd by 1840.

The original states slid

Virginia fell from 1st to 17th, South Carolina from 7th to 24th, and Connecticut from 8th to 29th. They kept growing, just more slowly than the West.

The centre walked west

The estimated centre of population moved about 9.4° of longitude, roughly 500 miles, almost all of it due west along the 39th parallel.
