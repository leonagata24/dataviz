¿Por qué México?

The fact sheet says who buys Minnesota's turkey but not why. The pugs framed three funny hypotheses and then checked each against sources. Press a button to reveal each verdict. Everything in the verdicts comes from outside the dataset, so each claim is linked to its source and graded in the evidence ledger below.

Hypothesis 1 of 3

“The turkeys are going home.”

Turkeys are native to Mexico (and the United States), where they are called guajolotes. The pugs figure the birds are simply returning to their roots, like a very large, very feathered family reunion.

Funny, but backwards

Mexico buys its turkey instead of raising it. Per-person turkey consumption there is only about 1.25 kg a year, roughly 90% of it eaten around Christmas, and only about 6% of the birds on Mexican tables are raised in Mexico (Tridge, relaying a UNAM statement reported by Milenio, Dec. 2024). A separate report calls turkey farming in Mexico nearly nonexistent and says more than 90% of the turkey Mexicans eat comes from the United States (FERN, undated). A native bird, mostly imported.

Hypothesis 2 of 3

“Mexico is buying millions of whole holiday birds.”

The pugs picture a giant Christmas-dinner pipeline: 39 million turkeys in, roast dinners out, and a lot of gravy.

Busted

The big business is not the roast. In a USDA Economic Research Service briefing, fewer than 1% of U.S. turkey exports were whole birds. Most shipments were lower-valued parts or ground and mechanically deboned meat, which importers mix with other meats in sausage (USDA ERS briefing, web-archived Dec. 2008). A USDA report from November 2007 says Mexico's processing industry imports turkey cuts and mechanically separated turkey for its cold cuts industry, because domestic production cannot meet demand (republished by The Poultry Site). Pug summary, with the dating caveat below: Mexico's turkey imports have mainly fed its sausage and cold-cut makers, not a market for roast birds.

Both sources are old (2007 and 2008), and the ERS figure covers all U.S. exports, not only Mexico. A USDA attaché report on Mexico, whose page shows no date, says Mexico will continue sourcing mechanically deboned turkey from the United States (USDA FAS GAIN).

Hypothesis 3 of 3

“It is just the neighbor with the open gate.”

Mexico is close, the paperwork is easy, and nobody has to carry anything across an ocean. The pugs call this the shortest walk to a customer.

Mostly confirmed
  • The gate is open by treaty, for now. NAFTA moved Mexico from strictly administered imports to tariff- and quota-free trade with the United States and Canada, and the USMCA, in force since July 1, 2020, largely continues those market access provisions (USDA ERS, 2024, on agricultural trade in general; it does not mention turkey). The National Turkey Federation calls the USMCA critical to maintaining U.S. turkey access and puts Mexico at 79% of U.S. turkey exports, citing a USDA outlook (NTF, year of the figure not stated). The treaty is under review: on July 1, 2026 the United States did not agree to renew it in its current form (USTR), which starts annual reviews, while the agreement stays in force until July 1, 2036 (IISD).
  • Few others could fill the order, at least once. In January 2014, the president of the U.S. poultry export council said the United States was the only country producing enough mechanically separated poultry to meet Mexican processors' demand (Poultry World). That is twelve years old, predates the USMCA, and comes from an industry voice with a stake in the answer, so the pugs treat it as history, not as a current fact.
  • Rivals exist, and they are gaining in cuts. Chile, the only competing supplier then, held about 6% of Mexico's market in 2015 (FERN, undated). Mexican producer-association data reported in January 2023 show Brazil's share of Mexico's turkey meat imports, excluding mechanically deboned meat, rising from 0.2% in 2021 to 8.2% in 2022 (through October), and Chile's from 10.5% to 15.5% (WATTAgNet). Older sources (the 2014 quote above and the undated USDA report) point to the neighbor's edge being strongest in mechanically deboned meat, which the 2022 figures exclude, so the pugs cannot say how big it is today.

Bonus twist: this is a stable niche, not a boom. Apparent turkey consumption in Mexico fell 21.5% between 2014 and 2023, to about 136,000 metric tons, and the advisory group GCMA attributes it to changing consumer preferences (The Poultry Site). Mexico has also been the leading buyer for years: a July 2018 USDA ERS note says more than half of U.S. turkey exports went there for most of the preceding decade, peaking at 74% in July 2014 (USDA ERS).

Verdict from the pugs: not homesick, not a holiday roast, and not an accident. A neighbor with a trade agreement and a taste for sausage.

The Mexico stress test: how far would the turkeys have to go?

Arithmetic, not a forecast
Mexico takes 71% of Minnesota's turkey export dollars from about 1,800 mi away, and 75% of all export dollars go to markets within 2,000 miles. If Mexico bought nothing and the other markets refilled the gap, the average mapped export dollar would travel about 2,500 mi instead of 1,910 mi, and only 14% of all export dollars would stay within 2,000 miles. Distances are straight lines to capitals, and the dollars are estimates.Mexico takes an estimated $73.8 million of Minnesota's $104 million in turkey exports. To refill that gap, every other market would need to buy about 3.5 times as much (3.3 to 3.6 once rounding is allowed for), and the new customers would be farther away. It is arithmetic, not a forecast, on data about three years old.

    How to read it: streams run in a straight line from Minnesota, and their width is proportional to estimated dollars. The number of little turkeys is only for show, so small markets still get a few. Rings show true distance from Minneapolis, because this map is centered there. The little turkeys move at the same speed everywhere, so a farther market takes longer to reach: that shows distance, not real transit times. Distances are straight lines from Minneapolis to each country's capital, not shipping routes; the fact sheet does not say where the turkey is shipped from or which ports it uses, and Minnesota's farms are spread across the state. “All other” (15% of dollars, many countries) cannot be placed, so the average distance and the within-2,000-miles share cover the named markets only. The other markets together are about $30.2 million today (29% of the total); replacing Mexico entirely takes about +245% growth from them (90% of the simulated roundings fall between +234% and +256%). The ranges on the readouts come from 3,000 simulated roundings, each share varied by up to ±0.5 point and rescaled to 100%; they show the 5th to 95th percentile. The fact sheet gives no price, volume or time dimension, so none of this says how likely a shift is or how quickly other markets could grow. Trade-agreement reviews, tariffs and avian flu are outside this dataset.

    What this can and can't tell you

    • It is one fact sheet, not a time series. Only 2022 and 2023 production are given. The sheet notes that exports fluctuated over the past decade because of avian flu but gives no numbers, so nothing here describes a trend in exports.
    • The “why” is outside evidence. The three verdicts rest on linked sources of uneven age and independence, graded in the ledger. They explain the pattern; they do not prove cause from this dataset.
    • Different measures, different bases. 13% of production exported (the measure is not stated), $104 million in export value, and “more than $1 billion” in cash receipts describe different things at different stages. The pugs did not divide one by another.
    • Shares are rounded to whole percents. A 2% market is anywhere from 1.5% to 2.5%. Every ranking among the 2% markets and between ties is noise.
    • Estimated dollars. Dollar values by country are share times $104 million, not reported trade statistics.
    • The stress test is a what-if. It scales Mexico's stream and, by default, spreads the lost dollars evenly over the other markets; it does not model prices, quantities, shipping routes or time. Map distances are straight lines to capital cities.
