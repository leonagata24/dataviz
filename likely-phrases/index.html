The Pug Oracle reads “likely”

Ask a hundred people what “likely” means and you get a hundred answers. This quiz asked a smaller question: of two phrases, which sounds more probable? About 5,000 people answered ten of those each, 51,740 answers in all. The pugs turned them into a ranking of 19 phrases, with error bars. The file has no percentages, so it cannot say what “likely” is worth in numbers; it can say where “likely” stands in the line, and who it stands next to. The full data quality audit is at the bottom.

CardIThe reframe

Is “likely” just “probable” in a nicer collar?

Statistically, yes251 of 473 people (53.1%) picked “Likely” over “Probable”; the 95% range runs from 48.6% to 57.5%, so a fair coin fits. Both phrases beat everything below “Very Good Chance”, and both lose to it.

The chart pits “Likely” against each of the other 18 phrases. Where the dot and its whisker sit entirely right of the 50% line, “Likely” wins that matchup; entirely left, it loses; spanning the line, the data cannot call it. Only Probable lands in the tie. “Very Good Chance” beats “Likely” 64% of the time (145 of 403 picked “Likely”), and “Probable” loses to it by a similar margin (74 of 256 picked “Probable”), so the two travel as a pair.

The pugs would like it noted that “Likely” and “Probable” have never once been seen sharing a treat.

CardIIWait, really?

When the crowd splits 53 to 47, is everyone flipping coins?

No, mostly two campsEveryone saw one pair twice, with the order swapped. For “Likely” vs “Probable”, 20% (23 of 116) changed their answer. A coin flip would change it about half the time; across all pairs, 3.4% did.

A 53/47 split can mean one of two things: everyone is unsure and guessing, or people hold firm but different views. Repeat answers tell them apart. The less lopsided the crowd on a pair, the more people changed their answer, but even in the closest pairs only about one in seven did. So most of the disagreement is between people, not inside them. “Likely” vs “Probable” is the pair with the most changed answers in the whole quiz (23), at 20%, above every grouped bar.

Source: pairwise comparisons file. Each of 5,174 respondents saw their first pair again as their tenth, with the phrases swapped. Bars: share who picked the other phrase the second time, with 95% Wilson ranges. Crowd split measured on all answers to that pair.

CardIIIWait, really?

How many different things can a pug actually say?

About eight19 phrases, but the crowd separates them into about eight groups. Within a group, neighbors are hard to tell apart; between groups there are clear gaps.

The ladder shows each phrase’s strength score, estimated from all its matchups at once, with a 95% range from 1,000 resamples of respondents. Scores are on a relative scale: 0 is the average phrase, and a gap of 1 means the higher phrase wins about three of four comparisons. It is a ranking, not a percentage. The ranges say where the order is solid (ranks 1 to 4, and 18 and 19) and where it is not: Likely and Probable share ranks 5 and 6, Might Happen and Could Happen share 11 and 12, Chances are Slight and Little Chance share 14 and 15, and Improbable and Remote Chance share 16 and 17.

Source: pairwise comparisons file. Bradley–Terry strength scores fitted to all 51,740 answers; ranges are the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles of 1,000 bootstrap resamples of respondents. Shaded bands group neighbors less than 0.9 score units apart, a judgment call.

Two details from the pairs behind the ladder. “Realistic Possibility” sits above “About Even”: 57.8% of people picked it as the higher one (107 of 185; range 50.6% to 64.7%), and “Better than Even” beats it only 58.8% of the time (218 of 371). And “Remote Chance” loses to “Little Chance” 80% of the time (189 of 235) yet is a statistical tie with “Improbable” (50.8%, 163 of 321).

What this can and can’t tell you

  • It ranks words; it does not convert them to percentages. The article’s second quiz part asked for 0 to 100% estimates, but that data is not in this file. So “Likely” has a place in line, not a probability.
  • The sample is a self-selected online quiz. No demographics, country or timestamps are in the file, so nothing here describes any population, and differences between groups cannot be tested.
  • Scores are relative. A strength score of 2.7 is not 2.7 times anything useful; only differences and order mean something.
  • The grouping into “about eight” is a rule of thumb. A gap threshold of 0.9 score units gives eight groups; a looser one gives fewer. The ranges, not the bands, carry the evidence.
  • Phrases appeared in uneven pairings. Matchups range from 127 to 634 answers, so raw win rates mislead (see the audit); the scores and head-to-head shares account for that.
  • One answer per person is repeated. Each respondent’s tenth answer repeats their first pair. It is counted in the ranking; dropping it leaves the order identical.
Audit
✓Data quality audit · one file, 51,740 rows

Does this data smell right?

Edible, with careThe file is complete and tidy, and the crowd is remarkably consistent. Its limits are what it leaves out (numbers, demographics, time) and an uneven pairing design.

Smells like a very well-run quiz. The pugs would still like to see the other half of it.
