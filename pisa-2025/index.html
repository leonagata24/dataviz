Did the Robot Do the Homework?

Candidates: countries and economies, 15-year-olds · Examiners: a panel of suspicious pugs

PISA 2025 tested 15-year-olds in science, mathematics and reading, and asked them about AI chatbots and phones in school. Scores are lower than in 2022 in most places: among the countries with comparable results, the median change in math was points and scored lower (the file does not say which changes are statistically significant). So this page asks the questions suspicious pugs would ask: is the chatbot to blame, do phone bans help, and does anything digital line up with scores at all? The short answer: across countries, neither AI use nor phone bans shows a detectable link with math scores, or with how those scores changed.

So does anything digital line up with scores?

[6 measures]
One of sixAnswerOnly schools' capacity to use digital devices tracks math scores (). The other five measures are within noise.

Prime suspect: resources. Evidence in this file: none.

Working

One thing does. Of the six digital measures in the dataset, only the index of schools' capacity to use digital devices tracks math scores, at , and it is the only one that survives a correction for testing six measures at once. The other five have ranges that straddle zero.

Read it with care. The index is built from principals' reports, and the dataset notes warn that countries are not directly comparable in its raw units. A rank correlation uses only the ordering, which softens that problem without removing it. One plausible reading is that capacity travels with how well-resourced a school system is, but this dataset has no income measure to test that. Inside the OECD the link is weaker, at .

Read before marking

What this can't tell us

  • Countries, not students. Each dot is a national average, so a flat line across countries says nothing about individual students. The OECD's own student-level analysis in the source report is more mixed: students who use AI for specific schoolwork tasks score lower in science than those who don't, while weekly users of AI for general learning score about the same as non-users.
  • Within noise is not proof of zero. The ranges show how large a link the data cannot rule out. For AI use and math that is anything from .
  • Correlation only. A country may ban phones because distraction was already a problem, which would hide any benefit. One snapshot cannot sort that out.
  • Self-reports. AI use and distraction come from student questionnaires and bans from principals. What counts as "using AI to learn" may differ by country.
  • Sampling flags. The OECD marks six systems where sampling standards were not fully met, and the dataset notes urge particular caution on questionnaire results for Albania and the United States. Removing all six does not change any conclusion here.
  • Significance. "Significant" means p < 0.05 on a Spearman rank test and a 95% bootstrap range that excludes zero; "borderline" means only one of the two holds. With about 85 countries, correlations smaller than roughly ±0.21 cannot be told apart from zero. About 30 tests were run for this page, so one or two chance hits would be expected at that threshold; the two headline positives (capacity with math, bans with distraction) both survive a strict correction for that.

Examiner's final remark No paw prints on the scoreboard. At country level the robot can be neither convicted nor cleared, and phone bans go with a little more quiet but no extra points. All this talk of homework is making us want a nap.
